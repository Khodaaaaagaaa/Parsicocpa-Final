Missing Cost Basis on Form 1099-B 2026 – Reporting Considerations for 2025 Investment Sales

PT
Parsi Team Content Group — Reviewed by Parsi Team, CPA
October 5, 2026
Last Reviewed: October 5, 2026
6 min read
Prepared by Parsi Team

This article was drafted by the Parsi Team content group and subjected to technical, legal, and compliance review and final approval by Parsi Team, CPA, prior to publication.

Missing cost basis on Form 1099-B generally indicates a gap in broker reporting or supporting information, rather than establishing that an investment had no acquisition cost. Because gain or loss depends on adjusted basis, an unresolved basis figure may materially affect the reported result of a stock sale.

This publication explains the distinction between sale proceeds, basis supplied by a broker, and basis reported to the IRS. It addresses 2025 investment sales reported during the 2026 filing season and includes California reporting considerations. The discussion concerns stocks and conventional brokerage securities, rather than digital-asset reporting. It is educational and general; no specific taxpayer facts or circumstances are evaluated.

What This Article Covers
  1. Missing basis and noncovered securities.
  2. Sale proceeds compared with adjusted basis.
  3. Records commonly associated with basis reconciliation.
  4. Form 8949 reporting categories and adjustments.
  5. California capital-gain treatment.

1. Missing Basis and Noncovered Securities

Form 1099-B may be delivered as part of a consolidated brokerage tax statement. The package can contain separate transaction groups based on holding period and whether basis was reported to the IRS.

For certain noncovered securities, the broker is permitted to leave acquisition-date and basis fields blank. A noncovered designation concerns the broker's reporting obligations; it does not establish that the sale is exempt from tax.

Statement entryGeneral interpretation
Basis reported to the IRSA basis figure was included in broker reporting; accuracy and adjustments remain relevant.
Basis provided but not reported to the IRSThe taxpayer's statement may contain useful basis information despite different IRS reporting treatment.
Basis unavailable or blankSupporting records may be necessary to establish the appropriate amount.

A blank field is not itself evidence of zero basis. Conversely, the presence of a number does not establish that every required adjustment has been reflected. These distinctions are relevant to the Form 8949 reporting framework.

2. Sale Proceeds Compared with Adjusted Basis

For a straightforward sale of purchased stock, gain or loss generally reflects the difference between the amount realized and adjusted basis. Purchased-property basis typically begins with acquisition cost; applicable costs and later adjustments can affect the calculation.

The following hypothetical example assumes that proceeds already reflect selling expenses, adjusted basis is supported by records, and no further adjustments apply.

Calculation elementHypothetical amount
Sale proceeds$25,000
Adjusted basis$18,000
Capital gain$7,000

Using zero in place of the supported basis would produce a $25,000 gain in this simplified calculation. The difference illustrates the effect of a reporting error, rather than a promised tax reduction. The example does not establish basis for an actual sale. Different treatment may apply to inherited property, gifts, employee-related shares, or transactions requiring other adjustments.

3. Records Commonly Associated with Basis Reconciliation

Records associated with a basis review commonly include purchase confirmations, historical account statements, reinvestment histories, transfer documentation, and notices of corporate actions. Their usefulness depends on whether they can be connected to the particular shares sold.

A statement showing the total value of an account may provide less information than transaction-level records. Similarly, a current account balance does not establish the acquisition cost of a historical sale.

When shares moved between brokers, the history held by the original institution may help explain information missing from the receiving institution's tax package. Reconciliation can involve the quantity transferred, acquisition information, and the particular shares later sold.

For inherited or gifted investments, ordinary purchase-cost assumptions may not apply. IRS Publication 551 describes inheritance and gift basis rules, including circumstances in which the basis used to calculate a gain differs from the basis used to calculate a loss. The value shown on the date of a transfer is not necessarily the applicable tax basis.

The documentation question is therefore more specific than whether an investor has a brokerage statement. It concerns whether the available evidence supports the basis and holding-period treatment of the reported disposition.

4. Form 8949 Reporting Categories and Adjustments

Form 8949 generally reconciles capital-asset transactions with information reported on Forms 1099-B and other applicable statements. The resulting totals are generally carried to Schedule D, subject to the relevant reporting exceptions.

The reporting approach depends partly on whether basis was reported to the IRS. An incorrect reported figure and an unreported basis figure can require different treatment. The instructions describe the relevant categories and adjustment procedures.

Holding-period classification is also relevant. Capital gains and losses are generally short-term when an asset was held one year or less and long-term when held more than one year, subject to exceptions. Acquisition information can therefore affect classification as well as the calculation itself.

⚠ Important Note

Missing broker information does not by itself establish that a sale may be omitted or that zero basis is appropriate. The reporting treatment depends on the transaction, available evidence, and applicable rules. The discussion here does not resolve an individual filing position.

5. California Capital-Gain Treatment

California does not provide preferential capital-gain tax rates. Capital gains are taxed at ordinary income rates for California personal income-tax purposes. The FTB identifies California Schedule D (540) as applicable when federal and California capital-gain treatment differs.

Basis accuracy is consequently relevant to both returns. Where federal and California adjustments differ, separate calculations may be necessary. A federal result does not, by itself, demonstrate that the state result is identical.

The broader filing framework is discussed in Tax Extensions 2026 and the October 15 individual filing checklist. The separate payment framework for current-year investment income is discussed in Quarterly Estimated Taxes 2026. Those articles address related issues beyond this publication's basis-reporting scope.

Key Takeaways
  • A blank basis field generally indicates unresolved information rather than establishing zero acquisition cost.
  • Sale proceeds and taxable gain are different amounts; adjusted basis affects the calculation.
  • Transaction-level records can be relevant when brokerage reporting is incomplete.
  • Form 8949 treatment depends partly on whether basis was reported to the IRS and whether adjustments are necessary.
  • California capital-gain rates and certain calculations can differ from federal treatment.
